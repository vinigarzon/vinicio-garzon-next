How to use this

Don't read. These are short lines to remember, not an essay. Look at the audience, point at the picture, talk about what you see.

[CLICK] = press → (or space). Some slides reveal in steps. Numbers/letters (1–6, A–D) turn overlays on and off on the two analysis slides. Click a frame on the contact sheet to open it big; Esc closes.

Handoffs are written at the end of each part: say the next person's name and step aside. Keep your own part inside its time — the slide counter at the bottom right helps.

Vocabulary the rubric wants (use it out loud): framing, exposure, depth of field, shutter speed, composition, balance, proportion, line, form, shape, texture, tonal contrast, rhythm, silhouette.

Backup: if the internet fails, the deck also runs from the folder (open index.html) and the video is replaced by the quotes on the next slide.

The life. The rules. The moment.

Slide 51:20 – 2:00

Before the camera,the line.

"He was born in 1908 near Paris, into a rich family. Cartier-Bresson thread was in every sewing kit in France. That money gave him freedom: he never had to sell pictures to eat."

"And here is the key: he did not start with a camera. He started with a brush. At eighteen he studied with André Lhote, a Cubist painter, who taught him geometry — the golden section, balance, proportion. You see this spiral? Keep it in mind. Every photo you will see today is built on it."

"Then Cambridge for a year: literature, painting, English. Still a painter."

Slide 62:00 – 2:25

Chance isthe teacher.

"Second influence: Surrealism. In Paris he sat with André Breton's group. Their idea was: walk the city, trust the unconscious, wait for the strange coincidence."

"Look at Brussels, 1932. One man peeks through the canvas; the other looks at us like we caught him. No story, no explanation — just a mysterious, funny moment. That is Surrealism with a camera."

Slide 72:25 – 3:00

One photographchanged his life.

"In 1930 he went to Africa, the Ivory Coast, to hunt. He got a fever and nearly died. When he came back, he saw this picture, by Martin Munkácsi: three boys running into Lake Tanganyika."

"His words: 'I suddenly understood that photography can fix eternity in a moment.' In 1932 he bought a Leica — a small camera you can hide in your hand — and he put the brushes down for forty years."

Slide 83:00 – 3:25

The painter's eye,at 1/100 s.

"Hyères, 1932. Look: the staircase is the spiral we saw before; the cyclist is a blur of speed. Geometry from Lhote, chance from the Surrealists. This is the recipe."

"He travels — Spain, Italy, Mexico. At twenty-five he has a solo show in New York; at twenty-seven he exhibits with Álvarez Bravo in Mexico City."

Slide 93:25 – 4:00

Declared dead.Came back.

"Then cinema: he works as assistant to Jean Renoir. Then war. In 1940 he is captured — thirty-five months as a prisoner. He escapes on the third try."

"1945, Dessau, a camp for refugees. The woman in black has just recognised the Gestapo informer who denounced her. Look at the faces around them. One frame — and the whole war is in it."

"And a crazy detail: in 1947 the Museum of Modern Art in New York prepared a posthumous show — they thought he was dead. He showed up to the opening."

Slide 104:00 – 4:30

The eye of the century.

"1947: with Robert Capa and three friends he founds Magnum, a cooperative owned by the photographers. He takes Asia — and he is there when history happens: Gandhi, ninety minutes before the assassination; the funeral for Life; the fall of the Kuomintang in China; the first Western photographer in Russia after Stalin."

"1952: his book The Decisive Moment, with a cover by Matisse. In the seventies he goes back to drawing. He dies in 2004, at ninety-five."

Handoff: "So: one camera, one lens, and a set of rules he almost never broke. Shun — what were the rules?"

Slide 124:40 – 6:00

Less equipment, more eye.

(Flip the cards one by one — ~15 seconds each.)

01 "One Leica, one 50 mm lens — the same angle of view as your eye. He covered the chrome with black tape to be invisible."

02 "No flash. He said flash was 'like coming to a concert with a pistol in your hand'. Only available light."

03 "No cropping. He printed the black edge of the negative to prove the full frame. Composition happens in the camera, not on the computer."

04 "No darkroom. A lab printed his work. For him the photo was finished when the shutter closed."

05 "Black and white — so you see line, shape, texture and tonal contrast, not colour."

06 "And the most important one: geometry first. He looked for the structure, and then waited for life to walk into it. Let me show you."

Slide 136:00 – 6:40

Children in the ruins

"Seville, 1933 — three years before the Spanish Civil War; this is just an old ruin. Click A: the broken wall is a frame within the frame. He painted his Leica black so the children would not notice him."

"B: the wall is in front, the kids are far — that is depth, and the small figures give proportion. C: texture — crumbling plaster against skin. D: hard Spanish light, strong tonal contrast."

"Structure first. Then the boy on crutches walks into the frame. That is the method."

Slide 146:40 – 8:30

A contact sheet of the 20th century.

"Now the work. This looks like a contact sheet — the strips photographers used to choose their pictures. I will open five. (Click a frame; ← → moves inside the lightbox; Esc closes.)"

Coronation, 1937: "Sent to photograph the king — he photographed the crowd. Everybody looks up; one man sleeps on the newspapers. The story is never where you expect."

Shanghai, 1948: "The last days of the Kuomintang. People crushed in a line to change paper money for gold. Some died. Look at the diagonal of bodies — chaos with structure."

Srinagar, 1948: "Women praying toward the Himalayas at sunrise. The arm opens like she is releasing the clouds. Balance: the figures on the left, the mountains on the right."

Rue Mouffetard, 1954: "A boy with two bottles of wine and the proudest face in Paris. Shallow depth of field — the street blurs, the boy is sharp."

Giacometti, 1961: "The sculptor, in the rain, coat over his head — and he looks exactly like his own thin sculptures. The vertical lines of the rain repeat him."

(Optional if time: Matisse, Marne, Simiane, Valencia.)

"Not one, but several Cartier-Bressons."

"How do critics organise fifty years of work? Clément Chéroux, curator of the big 2014 retrospective in Paris, says there is 'not one, but several Cartier-Bressons'."

"The Surrealist of the thirties. The engaged witness of the war years. And the Magnum eye who travelled the world — and then became quiet and went back to drawing."

Handoff: "He always said: 'I have never been a storyteller.' So what was he doing? Andy."

The decisivemoment.

"Thanks, Shun. My part is the idea behind the pictures — and then we go back to the man in the air."

Slide 179:10 – 9:40

Imageson the sly.

"In 1952 he published this book. The French title means 'images on the sly' — stolen images, like a street seller who runs when the police come. Cover by Matisse."

"The American publisher wanted something stronger, so he took a line from the epigraph, by a 17th-century cardinal: 'There is nothing in this world that does not have a decisive moment.' And that title — The Decisive Moment — became the most famous idea in photography. This is what it means."

Slide 189:40 – 10:15

Meaning

(Read the definition slowly.) "…the simultaneous recognition, in a fraction of a second, of the significance of an event, and of a precise organization of forms."

[CLICK] "Two halves. Meaning: something is happening. Geometry: at the same instant, the forms fall into place. Most photographers get one. He wanted both — in the same 1/100 of a second. And he said the camera should be like a sketchbook: 'an instrument of intuition and spontaneity'."

Slide 2011:45 – 12:10

Looking is everything.

"Three more things he said. 'Photographing is nothing — looking is everything.' 'I don't take the photograph, the photograph takes me.' And, at the end of his life: 'Photography is not a meditation. Drawing is.'"

"He even joked about his own myth: in a friend's copy of the book he changed the title to 'The More or Less Decisive Moments'."

Slide 2112:10 – 12:40

Critics &amp; curators

"The critics agree on one thing: the magic is not the camera. Kirstein, 1947: 'not in the mechanism in his hand but in the vision in his head.' Galassi, MoMA: 'clear, simple pictures… like a painting from the 17th century.'"

"And Capa's advice in 1947, which explains his whole career: 'Take the label of photojournalist, and keep the rest — the surrealist, the painter — deep in your heart.'"

"Now let's test the theory on one picture."

Slide 2212:40 – 14:10

Behind the Gare Saint-Lazare

"Back to our man. Press 1 to 6."

1 Timing: "The heel is one centimetre from the water. A fast shutter speed freezes him — the instant before the splash. That is the 'fraction of a second'."

2 Symmetry: "The water is completely still, so it works as a mirror. The man and his reflection share one axis — balance."

3 Echo: "Look at the wall: a poster of a dancer doing the same leap, reversed. Cartier-Bresson said he only noticed it afterwards. That is Surrealist chance."

4 Line: "The ladder in the water looks like railway tracks — we are behind a train station — and the poster spells 'RAILOWSKY'. The picture rhymes with itself."

5 Rhythm: "The barrel hoops are circles waiting — they anticipate the ripples that will come when he lands. Peter Galassi from MoMA says nobody ever made a better picture of this kind."

6 The exception: "He shot through a gap in a fence, too narrow for the lens. So the man who never cropped… cropped this one, on the left. He called it 'luck'."

Slide 2314:10 – 14:30

"So here is our conclusion: the decisive moment is not luck. It is being ready when luck arrives. Geometry first, then patience, then one hundredth of a second."

[CLICK] "TIME named it one of the 100 most influential photographs. A print sold for almost 600,000 dollars. And every one of us, waiting with a phone for the right instant, is doing what he did in 1932."
